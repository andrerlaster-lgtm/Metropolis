# Pocket Metropolis

A relaxing isometric tiny-city builder. You plan the city; residents decide for themselves where to live, work and stroll. Or sit back and watch: from a seed of a crossroads in 1850, the town grows by itself through the eras while you follow one family down the generations.

**What's in it**

- **Grow from a seed:** a new town can start as a little crossroads with a few cottages in 1850 and grow by itself (see *Growing through the eras* below). The first visit starts this way
- **Main person and generations:** you start by creating your main person. Follow them on the map, and when they die the story carries on with their child (or a niece or nephew), generation after generation
- **A neighbour run by the computer:** your town shares the world with a second town that the computer builds and runs by itself, from a small crossroads settlement up. It follows your rules: it pays for everything it builds, only builds what its era and milestones allow, and never touches your town. The World button shows both towns, who runs each, how they're doing and what the computer built lately; watch its town in 2D or 3D any time (see *The world and your neighbour* below)
- **Military base and raids:** the Base button opens your permanent military base, a separate 3D field with a command post. Build sandbag walls, machine-gun nests, bunkers and mortar pits (there must always be a way in for attackers), upgrade and repair them, recruit and train soldiers, post some on guard and keep the rest in reserve, all paid from a military budget that takes a share of the town's income. The computer's town trains its own troops and builds its own base from its own budget. While you're at war it raids your base in waves, with a warning a few hours ahead. Defend it yourself (send in squads, give orders, call artillery, pause to plan, or retreat) or leave it to your officers. You can attack its base too, from the War tab. Casualties, injuries, damage, costs and money taken are saved (see *The military base and raids* below)
- **Diplomacy:** each computer town has a mayor with a personality (warlike or peaceable, greedy or generous, honourable or two-faced, cautious or bold) that changes with what happens. War has to be declared, by you or by them. Propose peace, a non-aggression pact or an alliance with terms (money now, daily tribute, an army cap, how long it lasts); they accept, counter with what it would take, or refuse, and stop talking if you push too hard. They propose things too: peace when a war wears them down, your surrender when they're winning, friendship when they trust you. Breaking a treaty is a betrayal that costs you trust with every mayor (see *Diplomacy* below)
- **3D view:** the 3D button (or G) shows the same town in 3D. Orbit around it, walk the streets at eye level, or follow your main person or any resident you tap. Lighting follows the clock and weather, with shadows, lit windows, smoke, rain, snow and disasters. The game keeps running and saving underneath, and the builder is just as you left it when you go back (see *3D view* below)
- **Living streets:** people walk on the sidewalks, cross at zebra crossings (cars wait for them, and they wait for cars), cut through parks, and go in and out of buildings by the door. They head somewhere that suits the hour: work, school, the shops, a café, the park or a friend's house. Everyone looks a little different: height, clothes, hair, the odd hat, umbrellas in the rain
- **Town decisions** every couple of in-game days, with good and bad effects on how the town grows
- **Three save slots** (☰ → Save slots…): keep three towns, start a new town with a new main person in an empty slot, copy or delete, **export** a town as a file and **import** one back (to keep a copy, or move a town to another device; a file from a newer version or that isn't a town is turned away, and replacing a town asks twice). Opening Save slots also asks the browser to keep saves when space runs low. If a save fails (browser storage full or blocked), the game says so straight away, again every couple of minutes while it lasts, and once it works again; Save slots shows the warning too, and exporting the town you're playing takes it straight from memory, so the file has everything. A save that can't be read (damaged, or written by a newer version of the game) is never saved over: the game starts in an empty slot, says what happened and keeps a recovery copy. Only deleting the slot, starting a new town there or saving a copy over it replaces it. With the game open in two tabs, only one plays and saves; the other is paused with a **Play here instead** button, which has the first tab save and step aside, so tabs never save over each other

- **New town** (top-right button, or ☰ → New town…): choose **Sandbox** (everything unlocked) or **Milestones** (unlock buildings as you grow), a **small (16×16), medium (32×32) or large (48×48)** map, and **a seed** that grows, **empty land** or a ready-made **starter town**; whether it **grows by itself**; and **someone new** or the same family as your main person. Achievements carry over
- Homes (house, cottage, apartments, tower), workplaces (shop, café, bakery, office, mall, factory), services (school, clinic, library, fire station, university), recreation (playground, sports field), nature (tree, park, water) and roads. Factories pay well but their smoke makes nearby homes less desirable
- **Bakeries** are cheap 1850-era workplaces where residents grab a bite. **Libraries** (Village) add a little school coverage and a place to meet. **Fire stations** (Village) cover the homes around them: buildings there take up to 70% less damage from fires and lightning and 30% less from other extreme weather. A **university** (City) is a big employer whose school coverage reaches far. Each comes with its own Life Story career, from baker's helper to master baker, librarian, fire chief and university dean
- **Town budget:** building costs money (Sandbox builds for free). Residents and workers pay taxes, businesses pay per job filled, and roads, schools, clinics and parks cost upkeep. The balance and net per day sit in the stats bubble; the City panel's **Budget** shows what comes in and goes out by kind of building, the **top earning buildings** (with Show me), the last few days, and a balance trend. Undo refunds what an edit cost; the town can't go below zero
- **Notable people:** like great people in Civilization, a famous resident is born in or moves to your town when its buildings inspire them: schools inspire scientists, clinics doctors, businesses merchants, factories engineers, parks and cafés artists, sports fields athletes, and a growing town planners. Each arrival gets a card and a badge over their home, and gives the whole town a bonus (wider school or clinic reach, more tax, cheaper building, less upkeep, nicer streets). The City panel lists everyone and who is likely to come next
- **Service quality:** a school, library, university, clinic or fire station looks after only so many residents (a school 250, a clinic 320, a library 100, a fire station 280, a university 450). The residents near it are shared between the buildings that reach them, the nearer and bigger taking more. Past its limit a building's quality falls (never below 30%) and so does the coverage it gives, but only gradually: by up to 30% a day, and it climbs back the same way once there's room. The inspector shows a service's quality and how many it is looking after; the Services map view fades where a service is crowded and now shows fire cover too; residents ask for another when one is stretched; and a town that grows by itself builds one. Quality isn't saved: a loaded town starts each building at its current level
- **Town reputation:** how well the town is thought of, shown in the City panel under Era (Run-down, Plain, Pleasant, Lovely, Beloved). It's a slow score from how green and well kept the town is: trees and parks near homes, few damaged or ruined buildings and washed-out roads, and schools and clinics reaching the homes (only counted once you can build them). Planting helps and clearing hurts, but it moves by at most 4% a day, so it takes days to change, and a new title is written into the town history. A good name makes homes a little more appealing (up to ±5 points, "Town reputation" in the inspector) and a town that grows by itself grows up to 15% faster or slower. The panel says what is lifting or dragging it. It's saved with the town; a town saved before this existed starts at its current score, quietly
- Residents move into the most desirable homes. Desirability comes from parks and trees, water views, road access, school and clinic coverage, playgrounds and sport, jobs, and traffic
- **Milestones:** your town grows from Hamlet to Village (60 residents), Town (200), City (450) and Metropolis (900). Each title unlocks buildings for good, with a small celebration. Tap the stats bubble to see your progress
- **Resident thoughts:** residents say what they wish for ("Our kids have no school nearby") in bubbles over their homes and in the City panel, with a **Show me** button that flies to the spot. Issues are colour-coded: **red** needs fixing, **yellow** is worth a look, **green** is going well. While the City panel is open the affected tiles are tinted on the map in the same colour, and the issue you're looking at pulses with a marker over each tile
- Placing something shows how much nearby homes gained or lost (+6%), and a new home shows how appealing its lot is
- A labor market matches workers to the nearest jobs they can reach by road. Busy roads cost more to use, so commuters spread onto other routes when there are any, and cars follow those routes at morning and evening rush hour. Only through traffic counts towards a jam: a street isn't blamed for the people turning in and out of the buildings along it
- **Road suggestions:** when a street is jammed, the town tries out possible new roads behind the scenes (extending a dead end, a link road, a road alongside or around the jam) and residents only complain where one would really help. The suggestion is sketched on the map with a dashed outline, and **Build it** in the City panel lays it down in one step (undo works)
- **Street names and addresses:** every straight road gets a name (Maple Street, Lavender Avenue…) painted on the map when you zoom in, and every building gets a house number, odd on one side and even on the other. Names stay put as roads grow, and when a road is cut in two the longer part keeps the name. Life Story, the inspector and events all use addresses ("14 Maple Street") instead of map coordinates
- Inspect any tile to see its address, residents or jobs and a breakdown of its desirability
- Map views for desirability, traffic and services; pause and 1×, 3× or 10× speed
- A slow day/night cycle with lit windows, street lights and headlights
- Seasons (two in-game days each) and weather: rain with umbrellas and ripples, winter snow, morning fog
- Homes grow as people move in, towers and offices add floors as they fill, and roofs vary from house to house
- Gentle synthesised sound effects and ambience (birds by day, crickets at night, rain), with a mute switch
- Drag to build lines of road or paint trees, water and buildings; undo and redo every edit
- Autosave with versioned saves; older 12×12 towns open centred on the larger map
- **Share your town as a link** (☰ → Share town link). The map is packed into the link itself, so no server is needed. Opening a link gives the visitor a copy to play with. The visit is saved on its own, outside the save slots, so your towns are never touched; ☰ → Back to my town (or Save slots) takes you home, and saving a copy into an empty slot keeps the shared town
- **Minimap** in the corner: tap or drag to jump around the city (on by default on bigger screens, switchable in ☰)
- **Trends** in the City panel: population, happiness, employment and the busiest road over the last six in-game days, with a readout on hover or tap and a table view
- **Comfort settings** in ☰: colour-blind friendly colours (Okabe–Ito severity colours and blue-to-yellow map ramps) and larger text. They're remembered on this device
- **Life Story:** create a character who is born in your city and live their life through text events (see below)

Open `index.html` through any static server (ES modules need one, e.g. `npx serve .`), or build the single-file version:

```sh
node build.mjs   # writes dist/pocket-metropolis.html
```

## Growing through the eras

Inspired by [genesis](https://github.com/tan-zhuo/genesis): the town is something you watch grow, and technology changes what you see. Ideas only; no code was copied (that repository has no licence).

- **Calendar:** a town is founded in 1850 and a year passes every in-game day, the same pace as a Life Story life. The stats bubble shows the season, year and era.
- **Eras:** Pioneer days → Railway age → Motor age → Modern times → Bright future. Each needs enough people and enough years since founding (60 people and 15 years, then 180 and 35, 400 and 60, 800 and 90). A new era gets a card and an entry in the town history.
- **The look changes with the era:**
  - **Pioneer days:** dirt lanes with wheel ruts, lanterns on wooden posts, horse carts, timber walls and thatch-coloured roofs.
  - **Railway age:** cobbles, brick and clay roofs, gas lamps, and the first dark motor cars.
  - **Motor age:** asphalt with lane markings and electric street lights.
  - **Bright future:** solar panels on house roofs.
- **Let it grow** (on for seeds; ☰ → Town grows by itself): every so often the town makes one change from what its people need:
  - homes when houses are about as full as their appeal allows
  - shops, cafés, factories, offices and malls when people need work
  - a café, park or playground where townsfolk have nowhere nearby to eat, play or meet
  - a school or clinic where families have none
  - a new street when lots run out
  - now and then a tree
- **How it grows:** what it builds follows the era, from cottages to flats to towers. Streets grow in tidy blocks, because it keeps the way on from a dead end free and leaves a gap for a side street every five tiles. When the map is full, older homes near the middle are rebuilt bigger. Homes and businesses are free (residents build them); roads, services, parks and trees come out of the town budget. A happy town grows faster. You can keep building alongside it.
- **Town decisions:** every couple of days the town asks a question that fits the era and the moment: dig a well, clear the old woods, welcome newcomers, pay for the railway station, accept a mill, pave the roads, raise or cut taxes, take a gift of parkland, build a highway, allow towers, fund solar roofs, deal with a flood or a storm…
  - Each choice shows its effects: money, faster or slower growth, happier or unhappier homes (Town spirit in the inspector), the next era sooner, tax income, a new building, more or fewer trees, cheered-up townsfolk.
  - If nobody answers within a day, the last choice happens. **Later** tucks the question into a small pill, and a question that arrives during a Life Story event waits there too.
- **City panel → Era and Town history:** progress to the next era, how fast the town is growing and why, and a dated chronicle of firsts (first shop, school, factory, tower…), new eras, decisions and family births and deaths, with Show buttons.

## Your main person and their family

- A new town asks you to **create your main person**. **Follow** (top bar, or the Life panel on phones) keeps the camera on them, on the street when they're out and at home otherwise. Moving the map stops following.
- Grown-ups meet partners, marry and have children over the years.
- When the main person dies, their oldest living child carries the story on by itself after a few seconds (or you can tap Continue). With no children, a niece or nephew does. The new generation inherits some savings and the family home, and the town history records it.

## Save slots

☰ → **Save slots…** shows three slots, each with the town's era, year, population, map size and the main person you're following.

- **Play** switches to another town (the current one is saved first).
- **Start a new town** or **New town here** opens the New town sheet for that slot, with a new main person by default.
- **Save a copy here** keeps a snapshot of the current town.
- **Delete** asks twice.

The slot you're playing saves itself as you go. Towns saved before slots existed are in slot 1.

## Extreme weather

Weather runs from ordinary rain, snow and fog up to storms that can wreck the town.

| Weather | What it does |
|---|---|
| ⛈️ Storm | Lightning strikes a building now and then: cracks or a small fire. |
| 🥵 Heatwave | Hot haze; scorched lawns and the odd fire. |
| 🌨️ Blizzard | Snows roads in (no traffic) until it passes. |
| 🌊 Flood | Water spreads from rivers and lakes, washing out roads and soaking buildings. Low ground near water is at risk. |
| 🌪️ Tornado | A funnel crosses the map, flattening buildings and trees in its path to rubble. |
| 🌀 Hurricane | Long, town-wide wind and rain: damage everywhere, roads washed out. |

- **Warnings.** Floods, tornadoes and hurricanes come with a warning a little ahead. A decision card lets you pay to prepare (sandbags, shelters, boarding up), which halves the damage, or ride it out.
- **Damage.** A building with light damage works as normal; badly damaged ones hold half their residents and jobs; destroyed ones become rubble. Washed-out or snowed-in roads block traffic. Tap Inspect to see a tile's condition.
- **Repairs.** Crews repair one thing at a time while there's money. After a storm, a report card lists the damage with **Show me** and **Repair everything** (free in sandbox), and residents ask for repairs too. Building over rubble or a broken road also rebuilds it.
- **Decisions.** A building code (sturdier buildings) and a flood wall (floods do far less damage) come up as town decisions.
- **Setting.** ☰ → **Extreme weather**: Off, Gentle (default) or Wild. New towns are left alone until they have 25 people and a few years behind them, and extreme events are at least four days apart.

## Life Story

Open **Life** to create a character: name, pronouns, look and two traits. They are born as a baby into a family living in one of your city's homes, marked with a pin on the map.

- A year of life passes per in-game day in the background; **Age up** skips to the next birthday. Life time stops while an event waits for an answer.
- Events pop up as messages with two to four choices. Each choice has its own outcomes, which change Happiness, Health, Smarts, Looks, Money and relationships.
- The city shapes the life: schools and clinics near home, parks and trees, traffic on the commute, which workplaces are hiring and which homes have room. Bulldozing their home or workplace changes their story too.
- Stages run from baby to senior. When a life ends you can continue as your child, or start a new life.
- **Things to do:** between events, pick activities (two a year as a child, three from 13): study, exercise, see a doctor, hang out with friends and family, go on dates, visit the playground or park, go shopping, volunteer, babysit, work overtime, look for a job. Many use real places: the nearest sports field, playground, park, shop, school or clinic. Gains shrink as a stat gets high, so variety pays.
- **Buy a home:** save up and buy a real house or flat in your city. Prices follow desirability, so the homes you make lovely cost more. After a move the camera flies to the new home, the home pin shows its address, and tapping the address in the Life panel shows it on the map.
- **Neighbours** are the families living in real homes near yours, and they keep the same names. Events follow the city too: snow days, heatwaves, cherry blossom, a festival when your town reaches a new milestone, and nudges when there's no playground or school nearby.
- **Your character on the map:** from age 3 they walk their real routes, to work by day if they have a job or out to the park, with a ring and a marker so you can spot them.
- **Achievements** (18, kept across every life in the city) and a **life ribbon** for each finished life, such as Centenarian, Tycoon, Genius or Family first.

### Legacy events: a family through the town's history

Life Story events also follow your family through the town's history (`src/legacyEvents.js`, 49 events, with the rules in `src/legacy.js` and `src/lifeEngine.js`):

- **They depend on the era, on leading the town, on war and on the town.** Some events only happen in their era: gold fever in pioneer days, gas lamps and railway shares in the railway age, the first motorcar, radio nights and the stock market, solar co-ops. Others need a leader:
  - **When you're mayor:** council budgets, petitions, a bribe, a rival candidate, a factory strike.
  - **When a parent or relative is mayor:** being the mayor's kid, sitting in on the council.
  - **War:** the call-up, raid nights, letters from the front and a peace parade.
  - **The base:** reviewing the troops, dinner with the neighbour's envoys.
  - **The town:** a fair, newcomers, a fever when there's no clinic, a soup kitchen when jobs run short.
- **Six kinds:** personal, family, politics, money & work, war & the base, and town life. The card shows which.
- **Some choices reach the whole town:** town money, how happy homes are for a few days, trust with the neighbouring town, and soldiers' experience. The result card shows those changes too.
- **Consequences come back years, or generations, later.** Returning a found wallet, burying a family secret, planting an oak for the grandchildren, taking a builder's envelope, buying railway shares, suing the neighbours over a fence or opening a family shop can each return 3 to 45 years on. That might be to you, or to your child or grandchild. The card says who set it in motion ("↺ Set in motion by your grandparent Ada in 1872"), and the outcome follows what was chosen back then. A different family starting a new life in the same town never inherits them.
- **Less repetition:** the family remembers what it has lived through. An event that just happened is very unlikely for the next few, kinds of event take turns, a legacy event comes back to the same person at most every 12 years, and some happen once in the whole family line (the heirloom watch, the oak, the family letters).
- **Auto mode:** with your main person's **Auto** on, pick what happens with life events: **Ask me** (the default: they wait for you), **Play it safe**, **Be bold** or **Follow the focus**. Which job to take and which home to buy always wait for you.

The family's memory is saved in your town (`city.systems.legacy`: events seen, recent events, consequences waiting, heirlooms and a few named memories). Older saves start with an empty memory.

Events are plain data in `src/lifeEvents.js` and `src/lifeEventsMore.js` (78 in all) and `src/legacyEvents.js`; activities are in `src/lifeActivities.js` and achievements in `src/lifeAchievements.js`. See the comment at the top of each file to add more.

## The world and your neighbour

Your town is one of two in a shared world. The other is run by the computer: it starts as a small crossroads settlement and grows by itself while you play, at the same pace as your town (pausing pauses both). The two share the calendar, the clock and the seasons, but each has its own map, people, money, era, milestones and weather.

- **The World button** opens a map of both towns with whose each is (yours in lavender, the computer's in coral), their people, buildings, era, title and money, and the last few things the computer built.
- **Watch** puts the computer's town on screen. You can look around, open the map views and tap anything to see what it is, but the build tools are hidden: you only build in your own town. A banner says whose town it is and takes you back.
- **See in 3D** opens the 3D view on that town. In 3D every town stands where it sits in the world, with countryside and trees between, so you can fly from one to the other; the town on screen gets the walkers, cars, signs and street camera.

How the computer plays (`cpuPlanner.js`): it uses the same growth rules as a town that grows by itself (homes as they fill up, businesses when people need work, cafés and parks where townsfolk lack them, schools and clinics where families have none, new streets when lots run out), but it pays for everything at your prices, keeps a reserve for upkeep, and builds nothing it can't afford. When families need a school or clinic it can't yet afford, it saves up for it (opening the odd business meanwhile, since they pay taxes) and builds it when the money is there. It answers its own town decisions, and every placement goes through the same placement rules as your tools. Paying for homes makes it grow more slowly than a town that grows for free: expect a village with a school after a month or two of game days.

The world is saved inside your town's save (`city.systems.world`), so your save slots, exports and imports carry the neighbour too, and the save format version didn't change. A save from before the world existed gets a neighbour the first time it's opened; your town itself is left exactly as it was. More computer towns fit the same structure (`World.addNeighbour()` places them around yours); only one is created for now.

## 3D view

The 3D button opens the same town in 3D. It only reads the game: the simulation, autosave, Life Story and everything else keep running, and going back to the builder puts its camera where you were looking. You can't build in 3D; it's for looking around.

- **Orbit** circles a point on the ground. **Street** puts you at eye height on the nearest sidewalk; you can walk anywhere except into buildings. **Follow** rides along behind your main person (or, without one, someone nearby). Tap anyone to follow them instead; when they go indoors, the camera picks up someone else walking nearby.
- It's drawn in a **storybook look** (the overhead builder keeps its own look): houses are plaster cottages with timber framing, shuttered windows with flower boxes, steep shingled roofs and stone chimneys; cottages have stone walls under golden thatch; apartments, towers and offices are tall timber-framed townhouses whose upper storeys jut out, with dormers; shops, cafés and bakeries have shop windows, striped awnings and hanging signs. Homes get picket fences, stepping stones and flower beds; trees are lush and sway in the breeze; lawns have flowers, bushes and tufts. Streets are cobbled across the whole road, with raised flagstone sidewalks on the same edges the overhead view draws them, pale stone crossings where people cross and lanterns where the street lamps stand, so people and cars stay where the game puts them. Buildings keep the same growth stages, storeys and colours (deepened into storybook ones). Factories, schools, the clinic, the library, the fire station, the university, the mall, playgrounds, sports fields and rubble don't have storybook models yet and keep their earlier models under the new light.
- The textures (shingles, thatch, plaster, stone, cobbles, planks, bark, leaves, grass) are painted in code when the 3D view opens; nothing is downloaded.
- Light follows the clock and weather: a warm sun crosses the sky and casts soft shadows, windows glow softly by day and light up at night, lanterns glow (the nearest few cast real light), and there's fog, rain, snow, clouds and chimney smoke. Floods, fires and tornadoes show up as they do in the builder.
- **Quality** (the button by the camera modes): Auto starts at High on computers and Medium on phones and drops a level if frames stay slow; Low turns shadows and lamp lights off. The choice is remembered on this device (`pocket-metropolis:view3d` in localStorage, apart from saves).

How it's built: `src/view3d/` holds the 3D code and a copy of Three.js. The build copies it to `dist/view3d/`, and the page loads it only when the 3D view is first opened (it starts fetching when the pointer reaches the 3D button), so the game itself loads no slower. The town is cut into 8×8-tile chunks; each chunk is a few merged meshes rebuilt only when something in it changes (at most one per frame). People and cars are instanced, so a whole crowd costs a handful of draw calls. If the browser can't do WebGL, the game says so and stays in the builder.

## Townsfolk

About one resident in eight is a named townsperson who lives a day of their own. The ideas come from [emergent-city](https://github.com/leatag/emergent-city) (MIT), adapted to a cozy town: no crime or factions, just friendships, favourite cafés and weddings.

- **Personality and quirks:** each person has five traits (curious, hard-working, outgoing, kind, worrier, or their opposites) and a quirk such as Early bird, Night owl, Foodie, Homebody, Sporty or Shopper.
- **Needs** for rest, food, company and fun run down every hour. A small utility AI scores what to do next from those needs, their personality, the time of day, weekends and the weather: sleep, work (weekdays 8 to 5), eat at a café, shop or mall, meet friends, play at a park, playground or field, shop, or stay in.
- **Places come from your city.** People only go somewhere within about 12 tiles of home. When there's nowhere to eat, play or meet nearby, they say so in the City panel, and the homes are tinted on the map.
- **Relationships:** people at the same place at the same time meet. Similar, kind people click and become friends; close friends may fall in love and later marry; the odd pair falls out. Regulars pick a favourite café or park.
- **Notable people are townsfolk too.** Meeting one makes someone's day.
- **Hard weeks:** someone unhappy for a long stretch gets a visit from a friend, or after two days moves away.
- **On the map,** townsfolk walk between places with a mood dot over their head (green, yellow or red). Inspect a home to see who lives there and what they're doing; inspect a café or park to see who's there now.
- **City panel → Townsfolk:** town mood, what everyone is doing right now, the latest town news with Show me, and Meet the townsfolk (traits, needs, friends, partner, favourite place, latest memory). Weddings, new couples and people moving away also pop up as messages.

## The military base and raids

Your town has a permanent military base, on its own map: a 16 × 20 field with your command post near the bottom, the edge the enemy comes from at the top (red) and the area your reinforcements come in from at the bottom (lavender). It opens with the **Base** button and is drawn in 3D, from an angled camera behind your lines (or a free one you can turn).

- **Military budget.** A share of the town's daily income (20% to start, adjustable from 0 to 50%) moves into the military budget by itself, and **Budget → Emergency transfer** moves more over at once. Everything military is paid from it: defences, upgrades, repairs, recruits, artillery and the soldiers' daily pay. If it runs dry the troops go unpaid, and their morale suffers in battle.
- **Defences.** Sandbag walls (cheap; they steer attackers and give your soldiers cover), machine-gun nests, bunkers and mortar pits, each with three levels. They can go anywhere outside the enemy's edge and the reinforcement area, as long as attackers still have a way to the command post: the last way in can't be closed. Damage stays after a battle. Repair crews fix it over time in the order shown under **Repairs** (move what matters most to the top), or pay half as much again to fix something at once. Upgrading the command post lets the base hold more soldiers and post more on guard.
- **Soldiers.** Riflemen, machine gunners and grenadiers train for a few hours, or join at once as emergency recruits for 2.5 times the price. Medics are specialists, hired ready. Post soldiers on guard by picking one and tapping the field; the rest are the reserve, sent in during a battle as squads of up to four. Soldiers gain experience and rank (Private to Veteran, each rank a little tougher and harder hitting), get hurt and recover, and some fall; the fallen are remembered under **Reports**.
- **Raids.** The computer's town has a military too, funded from its own income and trained by the same rules. Only while you're at war with it (see *Diplomacy*), when it has the troops, and not in the first days of your base or while your base is protected after a battle, it announces a raid a few hours ahead (a toast, a red mark on the Base button, and the date in the base screen). When the raid arrives the whole world waits while you choose: **Defend the base** yourself, or **leave it to your officers** (the same battle, fought out by the computer for you). **Budget → Raids** sets how often: off, gentle (the default) or fierce.
- **Battles.** The raid comes in two or three waves. Your guards hold their posts and step out to meet anyone close; defences fire at whoever is nearest the command post. Send reserve squads in (one every 10 seconds), pick a squad and tap the field to move it, or tell it to hold, charge or fall back; call an artillery strike for $120 (one every 45 seconds). Pause at any time to plan: orders given while paused are carried out when you resume. You win when every wave is beaten or withdraws (a wave pulls back when most of it has fallen; the whole attack withdraws when its morale breaks or after five minutes). You lose only if your defending army is defeated (every soldier down, no reserve left and every armed defence destroyed), if its morale breaks and it flees, or if you retreat. Losing the command post doesn't end the battle on its own, but it's a heavy blow to your troops' morale. A battle left half-way is saved and can be resumed or finished by your officers.
- **Attacking.** The **War** tab shows what you know of each neighbour's base (about how many soldiers, how many on guard, how many defences), how you stand with them, and, while you're at war, an **Attack…** button (**Diplomacy…** declares war or makes peace). Your reserve goes (your guards too if you tick the box); you command the attacking squads, sending each in at the left, middle or right of their edge. Their guards hold their posts, their reserve comes out to meet you and their defences fire. Two **forward positions** sit in the middle of every base: stand on one with nobody of theirs nearby for a few seconds to take it (it lowers their morale), and your next squads can come in there. You win only by defeating their army (their soldiers, reserve and armed defences) or making it flee; destroying their command post doesn't win on its own, but it shakes their morale and often makes them break. Your attack withdraws when its morale breaks or after five minutes, when its supplies run out. Leaving it to your officers fights it out for you. Winning carries off part of their military budget into yours; either way their base is left alone for a few days and your troops rest for a day.
- **The computer's base.** It is built the way you'd build yours: from its own military budget it saves up for machine-gun nests, bunkers and mortars beside the way attackers would come, sandbags on that way to make them walk further, and posts a couple of guards beside its defences, keeping the rest of its soldiers free to raid.
- **Afterwards.** The report lists both sides' losses, who was killed, badly hurt (a few days to recover) or lightly hurt (a day), promotions, damage to the base and what it cost. A raid that wins carries off some of your military budget (and a little of the town's money) to the raider's budget. Your base is then protected from raids for a few days. Battles are written into the town history.

Raids and attacks only happen in a declared war (see *Diplomacy* below).

Nothing happens while the game is closed: raids are only planned and fought while you play. The military is saved in each town (`city.systems.military`, plus `city.systems.war` in yours for the raids); older saves gain a base the first time they're opened.

## Diplomacy

Open it from the **World** panel (**Diplomacy** on a neighbour's card) or from **Base → War → Diplomacy…**.

- **Mayors.** Every computer town is run by a mayor with four traits, each from 0 to 1: aggression, greed, honour and caution. The diplomacy sheet describes them in words (warlike, greedy, two-faced, cautious…). They change with events: winning battles makes a mayor bolder, losing makes them careful, a war they were losing teaches them caution, being betrayed makes them warier, long wars harden them and long peace softens them.
- **Where you stand.** At war, no treaty, at peace (the few days of protection after a peace treaty, when neither side may start a war), a non-aggression pact, or allies. Each mayor has a **trust** in you from −100 to 100: it grows while a peace, pact or alliance holds and with gifts, and falls with war, refused proposals and attacks. Your **reputation** (start 60) is what every mayor thinks of your word: breaking a treaty costs 25 points and some trust with every other mayor, and a poor reputation makes them reluctant to sign pacts or alliances.
- **War.** Raids and attacks only happen in a declared war. You declare it from the diplomacy sheet (tap twice; it warns you if it would break a treaty). A mayor declares war on you when they're aggressive, greedy for your wealth, don't trust you and think they're strong enough; never in your base's first days, never with **Base → Budget → Raids** set to off, and less readily for a while after a peace. Their first raid comes at least half a day later. Each war keeps a score: battles won, soldiers lost, money carried off.
- **Negotiation.** **Propose a treaty…** lets you set the terms: money paid now (either way), a daily tribute for some days (either way), an army cap on them or on you for some days, and how long it lasts (for peace: how long neither side may start a war). The mayor weighs it by their personality, their trust in you, your reputation and how the war is going, and accepts, makes a **counteroffer** with the terms they'd sign (take it, change it, or walk away), or refuses. After three proposals they won't take, they stop talking for a day.
- **Their proposals.** A card pops up when a mayor proposes something: peace when the war wears them down (paying you if they're the ones losing), your **surrender** when they've won at least two battles and are greedy (you pay, and your army is capped for a while), a pact when they trust you a little, an alliance when they trust you a lot. Accept, counter or turn it down; a proposal stands for a day, and they wait a few days before repeating a demand you refused.
- **Peace.** A peace treaty ends the war, turns back raids still on the way, and protects both bases for its protection days. Its terms are kept: tribute flows day by day, and an army cap stops recruiting above it until it ends.
- **Gifts** of $250 or $1,000 from the town's budget raise trust (more with a greedy mayor) and soothe grievances. An alliance can be ended without a war.

Diplomacy is saved in your town (`city.systems.diplomacy`) and each mayor in their own town (`city.systems.leader`). A save from before diplomacy starts at war with a neighbour that was already raiding or fighting you, and with no treaty otherwise.

## The mayor

Your town has a leader you control: at first your main person's father. Open the **Town hall** from the **🏛** chip on the town card (it shows how many actions are left today) or from **Mayor** in the City panel. The Life panel marks him as *Parent · Mayor*.

- **Skills:** charisma, business, command and diplomacy, from 0 to 100. Each duty works better with its skill, and the skill grows by 2 every time it's used.
- **Three actions a day**, and each duty once a day:
  - **Give a speech** (charisma): homes all over town are happier for two days (through the town's spirit, like a town decision).
  - **Hold a fundraiser** (business): money for the town, more as it grows.
  - **Inspect the base** (command): every fit soldier gains experience, and damaged defences get patched up.
  - **Meet a neighbour's mayor** (diplomacy): the neighbour that trusts you least trusts you more and holds fewer grudges (half as much during a war).
- **The family keeps the office.** The father ages with your main person's family. When he dies, your main person becomes mayor if they're an adult; until then an aunt or uncle is **acting mayor** and hands over when your main person turns 18. When you die as mayor, your heir takes over the same way. Each new leader starts with fresh skills; your main person's come from their own stats.

- **Growing up counts.** How your main person grows up decides how good a mayor they'll be. Looks, happiness and friends make charisma; smarts, a career and savings make business sense; health makes command; smarts, a settled mood and a partner make a diplomat; their traits add a little. While someone else leads, the Town hall shows **If … led today** with the skills they'd bring now.
- **Auto mode** for both of your people. In the Town hall, switch the mayor's **Auto mode** on and pick a focus (Balanced, Money, Happiness, Military or Diplomacy): the mayor does the day's three duties on their own, spread over the day, starting with the focus. In the Life panel's **Things to do**, switch **Auto** on and pick a focus (Balanced, Future leader, Smarts, Health, Happiness or Money): your main person does their own activities, spread through the year, choosing the ones that help the focus most (never job hunting or buying a home, which stay your choice). You can still act by hand while auto mode is on, and life events still wait for your answer.

The mayor is saved in your town (`city.systems.mayor`), auto mode in `city.systems.autopilot`. A town without a main person has a founding mayor until one is born.

## Town upgrades

A skill tree for your town. Open it from the **★** chip on the town card (it shows when you have points to spend) or from **Town upgrades** in the City panel.

- **Points** come from growing: one to start, one for each new town title (Village, Town, City, Metropolis), and one for every 50 residents at the town's biggest. People moving out never takes a point away.
- **Four branches**, each a chain of six upgrades where every upgrade needs one of the upgrades before it. The last upgrade in each branch costs 2 points.
  - **Treasury:** home taxes +10%, business taxes +15% (twice), building costs −10%, upkeep −20%, and Town bonds ($25 a day plus 25¢ per resident).
  - **Wellbeing:** parks and trees count 25% more, busy roads and factory smoke bother homes 40% less, playgrounds and sports fields count 50% more, and every home happier (+3% and +5%).
  - **Services & growth:** schools and clinics reach 1 tile further, playgrounds and sports fields 1 tile further, fire stations 2 tiles further; homes hold 10% more people, workplaces offer 10% more jobs, and newcomers move in 50% faster.
  - **Defence:** recruits train 30% faster, defences and their upgrades cost 20% less, the wounded recover 40% faster, the base is repaired 50% faster, soldiers' pay is 20% less, and new recruits start as Corporals.
- **Changing your mind is free.** Tap an upgrade you have to give its point back (once nothing after it needs it), or **Reset all**.

Upgrades are saved in your town (`city.systems.skills`: what you've taken and the most residents you've had). Older saves start with no upgrades and the points their town has already earned.

### Extending it

- **New desirability factors** (services, jobs, pollution, …): `FACTORS` in `desirability.js` already lists them as disabled stubs. Implement `compute(city, tile)` and set `enabled: true`.
- **New structures or zones**: add an entry to `STRUCTURES` in `config.js`, a draw routine in `renderer.js`, and a tool in `TOOL_GROUPS`. To gate it behind a milestone, list it in that milestone's `unlocks`.
- **New resident thoughts**: add a rule to `computeThoughts` in `advisor.js` with a priority, text, hint and tile.
- **Larger maps**: `CityState` takes any width/height and has `resize()`; the renderer culls to the visible tiles.
- **New saved data**: store it under `city.systems.<name>`, bump `SAVE_VERSION` and add a `MIGRATIONS[n]` step.
